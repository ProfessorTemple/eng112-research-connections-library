2. Shape your search

Start with one chosen keyword, then try combining two or three distinct concepts. AND usually narrows a search; OR usually broadens it. Put synonyms in the alternative fields. Your searches remain editable, and different PCC databases may interpret operators differently.

Open the ENG 112 PCC Library guide ↗ (internet and PCC login may be required).

Search operators: AND, OR, NOT, quotes, parentheses

AND narrows by requiring both concepts. OR broadens by allowing alternatives. NOT is available only through the separate exclusion field, since it can hide important results. Quotes search an exact phrase where the database supports it, and parentheses keep alternatives grouped. Database behavior varies.

Example: ("grandmothers" OR "intergenerational knowledge") AND gardening

Too many or too few results?

Too many: add one specific concept using AND, or use a phrase in quotation marks. Too few: remove one concept, add synonyms with OR, or try broader terms. Check article abstracts for better vocabulary. The program does not query PCC databases or verify subject headings.

Living Three-Story Thesis Studio

Begin with what you noticed. Move from a particular observation to an interpretation another reader could question, then to a larger significance your evidence can support. Your thesis is allowed to change as you read and listen.

My developing three-story movement

These are layers of thinking, not three mandatory essay sections. Each story must grow from the one before it. A research question is not yet a thesis; an interpretation makes a meaningful claim that can be investigated.

Give readers something particular to stand on: a scene, a pattern, a place, a detail, or a documented finding.

This is the intellectual turn. Offer an interpretation someone could reasonably question, and one your sources can help you investigate.

Open the view to a human, cultural, ecological, ethical, or philosophical implication. Avoid a grand claim unsupported by your observation and evidence.

Keep the questions alive. A living thesis can acknowledge what research cannot yet establish. Ground each interpretation in actual evidence; the third story is an earned implication, not automatically a call to action.

The Living Thesis Studio

Bring your three levels together in your own voice. A strong thesis may take two to four connected sentences near the end of your opening section, or emerge through the movement of creative nonfiction. It is not three separate assignments.

The program does not invent claims, verify sources, or write your interpretation for you. It only brings together words you have written; you must revise the resulting draft.

My working three-story thesis

Use your three stories to make a rough draft, then revise it into connected language. You can also write directly here. This is your own interpretation, not an automatically verified answer.

How can I tell if this is really a three-story thesis?

First story: Is there a particular observation or relationship, rather than just a broad topic?

Second story: Does my writing offer an interpretation that a thoughtful reader might question, or am I merely describing the first story again?

Third story: Does the larger significance grow from the interpretation, or have I made a sweeping claim that my evidence cannot support?

As a whole: Can I read the thesis as a coherent thought? Does it invite inquiry rather than announce certainty I have not earned?

Example: a three-story thesis growing from a creek encounter

First story: I had walked past the neighborhood creek for years without noticing the animals and debris gathering along its banks.

Second story: Hearing my father's memories of that same creek complicated my idea of it as neglected scenery, revealing it as a place shaped by labor, childhood, and environmental change.

Third story: This shift matters because the places we overlook may already be part of our communities' identities, and recognizing those relationships can change what we consider worthy of care.

This is a model of movement, not wording to copy. Its interpretation and significance must be supported by the particular writer's experience and evidence.
